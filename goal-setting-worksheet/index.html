How to set a goal you will actually keep

Most goals do not fail because people are lazy. They fail because the goal is vague, the reason is thin, or nothing was planned for the day it gets hard. This worksheet is built around those three problems. Fill it in once, put it where you will see it, and come back to it every week.

  1. Say what you want in one sentence. “Be healthier” is a direction. “Walk 20 minutes, three times a week, until March” is a goal.
  2. Write why it matters. Your reason is what you reach for when motivation drops. Make it yours, not what you think you should say.
  3. Describe success. What will you see, have or feel when it is done? A clear picture helps you know when you have arrived.
  4. Name the obstacles. Pick the two or three things most likely to get in your way, and write an if-then plan for each.
  5. Choose the first three steps. Small and close. The first one should take less than a day.
  6. Add a weekly tiny action. Something so small you can do it in your worst week.
  7. Add a deadline and an affirmation line to read each morning.

SMART goals, in plain words

SMART is an old and useful checklist. Each letter is a question you ask of your goal:

  • Specific: exactly what do I want?
  • Measurable: how will I know I have done it?
  • Achievable: is it realistic for my time, money and energy right now?
  • Relevant: does it matter to me, or am I doing it for someone else?
  • Time-bound: by when?

SMART makes a goal clear. What it does not do is help you on the Tuesday you are tired and would rather not. That is where the next section comes in.

WOOP and if-then plans, honestly explained

Psychologist Gabriele Oettingen has studied a method called mental contrasting, often taught as WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. You name your wish, picture the best result, then picture the real obstacle inside you or around you, then make a plan for it. Her work, along with research on “implementation intentions” (plans in the form if X happens, then I will do Y), suggests that this can help people follow through better than positive thinking on its own.

To be fair about it: no method guarantees results, and studies differ in size and quality. But the idea makes everyday sense. If you decide now what you will do when it rains, you do not have to decide while it is raining. That is why the worksheet has three if-then lines. They cost five minutes and remove a few excuses in advance.

A worked example

Press See an example to load this one into the sheet above.

  • Goal: Run 5 km without stopping by 31 March 2027.
  • Why: I want more energy for my family and to feel strong in my body.
  • Success looks like: I jog 5 km on a Saturday morning in about 35 minutes and feel good afterwards.
  • If it rains, then I do a 20-minute indoor workout. If I feel too tired, then I put my shoes on and do just 10 minutes. If I skip a day, then I go the next day (never miss twice).
  • First 3 steps: buy running shoes by Sunday; walk or jog 20 minutes on Tuesday; pick a local 5 km event for March.
  • Weekly tiny action: three 20-minute sessions on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday at 7am.
  • Affirmation: I am someone who keeps showing up for my body.

Notice how each line is something you could do or check. That is what makes a goal workable.

Track it and review it

A worksheet is a plan, and a plan needs a check-in. Put a reminder in your calendar for the same time each week and ask three questions: what did I do, what got in the way, and what is my next small step? To see your weekly action at a glance, print a free printable habit tracker and tick it off each time. What the affirmation line is for

The last line on the sheet is a short sentence in the present tense that you read, or write, each day. It does not make a goal happen by itself. Its job is to keep the goal in front of you and nudge you toward the next small step. Keep it believable: “I am learning to run a little further each week” works better than a claim you do not yet believe. If you like writing it out, the 369 method turns it into a short daily routine of writing it 3 times in the morning, 6 in the afternoon and 9 at night.

Questions

Is this goal setting worksheet free to print?

Yes. It is free, needs no sign-up and works in your browser. Fill it in on screen and print it, or print it blank and write by hand. You can save it as a PDF too.

What is a SMART goal?

SMART is a checklist for a clear goal: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. In plain words, it says exactly what you want, how you will know you got there, that it is realistic, that it matters to you and by when. It is a good check, though it does not help much with the days you do not feel like it, which is why this worksheet adds if-then plans.

What is WOOP?

WOOP stands for Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. You name a goal, picture the best result, picture the real obstacle that could get in the way, then make an if-then plan for it. It comes from the research of psychologist Gabriele Oettingen on mental contrasting, which suggests that imagining both the wish and the obstacle can work better than positive thinking alone.

How many goals should I set at once?

One to three. Each goal takes attention, so a few clear ones usually beat a long list. Use one worksheet per goal, and if you are unsure which to start with, pick the one that would make the others easier.

Is my worksheet saved or sent anywhere?
